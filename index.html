How a brushed DC motor turns

Current through a coil turns the rotor's iron arms into electromagnets. Each arm is pulled toward the opposite pole of the permanent magnets in the housing and pushed away from the like pole. That pull produces torque.

Left alone, an arm would stop once it lines up with the magnet it is attracted to. The commutator prevents that: it is a split copper ring on the shaft, and the stationary carbon brushes rub against it. As the rotor turns, the brushes touch different segments, so the current in each coil reverses right when its arm lines up with a magnet. The attracted arm becomes repelled, and the rotation continues.

In the model, red pole shoes are currently north electromagnets and blue are south. Watch them flip as they pass the magnets.

Speed, torque and voltage

A spinning coil also acts as a generator. It produces a back-EMF that opposes the supply and grows with speed:

V = I·R + Kₑ·ω and τ = Kₜ·I

At stall (ω = 0) there is no back-EMF, so current and torque are at their maximum. As speed rises, the back-EMF eats into the supply voltage, current drops, and the motor settles where its torque matches the load. More voltage means more speed; more load means more current.

Reversing the voltage reverses the current in every coil, so the motor spins the other way. Module 3 shows how an H-bridge does that from a single supply.

A servo is a tiny control system

Inside a hobby servo there is a small DC motor, a gear train, a potentiometer and a control board. The gears reduce speed by roughly 100 to 300 times and multiply torque by the same factor.

The potentiometer is attached to the output shaft, so its voltage tells the board the actual angle. The board compares that with the angle you asked for and drives the motor forward or backward until the error is near zero. That is closed-loop position control, the same idea you will tune yourself in module 4.

Telling a servo where to go

The commanded angle is encoded as the width of a pulse repeated every 20 ms (50 Hz). Around 1.0 ms means one end, 1.5 ms the middle, and 2.0 ms the other end. Many servos accept a wider 0.5 to 2.5 ms range for a full 180°.

The servo holds that angle as long as the pulses keep coming, and it resists if you push on the horn. Continuous-rotation servos use the same signal but treat it as a speed command instead.

Module 2 lets you generate the pulse and watch the horn follow.

Moving in exact steps

A stepper has no brushes. Its coils sit in the stationary stator, and the rotor is a permanent magnet. The driver energizes the coils in a fixed sequence, and the rotor snaps to line up with whichever stator poles are magnetized.

Each change in the sequence moves the rotor by one step, so the controller knows the position simply by counting steps. No encoder is needed as long as the load never forces the motor to skip a step.

This model uses a 2-pole rotor and 4 stator poles, so a full step is 90°. A typical NEMA 17 motor has a toothed rotor with 50 teeth, giving 200 steps of 1.8° per turn, but the principle is the same.

Duty cycle and average voltage

      
The period T is the time for one on-off cycle, and the frequency is f = 1/T. The duty cycle is the fraction of each period the pin spends high. Whatever is connected only feels the average when it is too slow to follow each pulse:

      V_avg = D × V_supply
      
On an Arduino Uno, analogWrite(pin, value) takes 0 to 255, so 128 gives about 50% duty. Pins 5 and 6 run near 980 Hz and the others near 490 Hz.

    

    

      
Why the frequency matters

      
The load has to smooth the pulses. Your eye averages an LED above roughly 60 to 100 Hz; below that you see flicker. A motor's rotor inertia averages the torque, and its winding inductance smooths the current.

      
Too low a frequency gives speed ripple and jerky motion; try 2 Hz on the motor tab. Too high, and the transistors spend more time switching, which wastes energy as heat. Motor drivers commonly run 15 to 25 kHz, just above hearing, so the motor does not whine.

    

    

      
Servos read the pulse width, not the average

      
A hobby servo expects one pulse every 20 ms. It ignores the duty cycle as such and measures how long each pulse lasts: 1.0 ms at one end, 1.5 ms at center, 2.0 ms at the other end (often 0.5 to 2.5 ms for a full 180°).

      
That is why you drive a servo with the Servo library instead of analogWrite: the library produces the right 50 Hz timing, and write(angle) converts the angle into a pulse width for you.

Why the diodes are there

      
A motor winding is an inductor, and current through an inductor cannot stop instantly. When all switches open, the winding forces its current to keep flowing and the voltage spikes until it finds a path.

      
The flyback diodes give it a safe path back to the supply, clamping the spike to about one diode drop above V+. Turn the motor on, then open all switches, and watch the brief diode current. MOSFET bridges often rely on the transistors' built-in body diodes for this.

    

    

      
How driver chips keep you safe

      
Chips like the L298N, L293D, TB6612 and DRV8833 hide the four switches behind logic inputs. Each IN pin controls one half-bridge: high closes the top switch, low closes the bottom one. The two switches on a side can never be on together, so shoot-through is impossible by design.

      
Speed comes from PWM on the enable pin (ENA). Real drivers also add dead time: a short pause between turning one switch off and the other on, because transistors switch off more slowly than they switch on.

      
The L298N uses bipolar transistors and drops about 2 V or more, so a 12 V supply gives the motor roughly 10 V. Modern MOSFET drivers lose much less.

What each term does

      u = Kp·e + Ki·∫e dt + Kd·de/dt
      
P pushes in proportion to the error now. More Kp is faster and stiffer, but past a point it overshoots and oscillates.

      
I adds up past error. It removes the small steady error that friction or a load leaves behind, but too much makes the response slow to settle and causes overshoot.

      
D reacts to how fast the error changes. It acts like a damper, braking before the target, which lets you use more Kp. It also amplifies encoder noise.

    

    

      
Reading speed from an encoder

      
The encoder gives counts, not speed. Speed is the change in counts divided by the sample time. With 11 pulses per motor turn, 4× quadrature decoding and a 30:1 gearbox, one output revolution is 1,320 counts.

      
At a 5 ms sample time, a single count corresponds to about 9 RPM at the output, so the speed reading jumps in 9 RPM steps. That quantization noise is why the speed tab includes a filter, and why the D term becomes useless unless you filter it. Try raising the encoder resolution or the sample time and watch the measured trace.

    

    

      
Practical details real code needs

      
Saturation and windup. The motor can never get more than ±12 V. If the integral keeps growing while the output is clamped, it overshoots badly once the error finally reverses. Anti-windup stops integrating while the output is saturated.

      
Derivative on measurement. Taking D from the measured value instead of the error avoids a huge spike, the derivative kick, every time the setpoint jumps.

      
Fixed sample time. Run the loop from a timer or check micros(), so dt is constant. Longer sample times add delay, which lowers the gain you can use before oscillation.

Why it falls, and the gain it takes to stop it

      
Gravity pulls a tilted pendulum further over, and the pull grows with the tilt. That is positive feedback: the system is unstable on its own. Accelerating the base toward the lean swings the pendulum back up.

      
Near upright, the angle loop needs a proportional gain larger than the total mass times g before it can win against gravity at all. The panel shows that threshold for the current masses. Below it the pendulum always falls; just above it, it falls slowly or oscillates.

      
Kd is not optional here. Without damping, P alone produces an oscillation that grows. Try setting Kd to zero.

Cascade control and the wrong-way move

      
The position loop does not push the cart directly. It chooses a small tilt target, and the fast angle loop tracks that tilt. To drive right, the pendulum must first lean right, which means the cart briefly moves left to tip it. Watch the cart step backward before it travels to a new target. Control engineers call this non-minimum-phase behavior.

      
A balancing robot is the same problem: the wheels are the cart and the body is the pendulum. Real robots measure tilt with an IMU (accelerometer plus gyroscope, fused with a complementary or Kalman filter), and they use wheel encoders for the position loop. Full state-feedback methods like LQR compute all four gains at once.
